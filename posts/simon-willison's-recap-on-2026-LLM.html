Notes on Simon Willison’s 2026 LLM Recap

October 23, 2026

I watched Simon Willison’s talk, 2026 in LLMs (so far), and wanted to write down what stood out to me.

The talk looks back at developments from November 2025 through September 2026. The recap below follows his annotated slides and accompanying notes, with my own experience added in the November and January sections. It summarizes selected themes rather than every event.

Sources: the original video and Simon’s annotated slides and notes. Dates and descriptions reflect the talk’s September 2026 perspective; later updates are linked where relevant.

Simon also has a soft spot for kākāpō, New Zealand’s flightless parrots. I’m mostly here for the LLMs, so I’ll leave the bird updates to him—apart from one pelican.

Jump to: Nov 2025 · Jan · Feb · Mar · Apr · May · Jun · Jul · Aug · Sep

November 2025: a turning point

When coding agents became more useful

GPT-5.1 was announced on November 12, followed by Claude Opus 4.5 on November 24. Simon sees this month as an inflection point: paired with their coding agents, the models became reliable enough for his everyday use.

That matches my own experience with Opus 4.5. It made my work easier, although I still needed to review the code carefully, especially on more complex tasks.

The pelican test

Simon tests models by asking them to generate an SVG of a pelican riding a bicycle. I guess one bird was unavoidable. It’s a playful test, not a comprehensive measure of a model’s abilities.

Neither model’s result was particularly convincing. Simon points out problems with the bicycle frames and the birds themselves. Two things stood out to me:

  • Useful coding ability didn’t guarantee a convincing SVG illustration.
  • A simple visual prompt made some of the models’ limitations easy to spot.

Warelay, before OpenClaw

Simon also highlights the first commit to Peter Steinberger’s Warelay repository on November 24, 2025. The project later became OpenClaw, passing through several names along the way. He documents that history in Warelay → OpenClaw.

January 2026: more ambition, mixed feelings

Taking on more projects

Simon changed his New Year’s resolution: instead of taking on fewer projects, he wanted to see how much more he could attempt with coding agents.

I don’t usually make New Year’s resolutions, but I’ve definitely started more projects this year. One is a macOS app called Switch, which changes the keyboard input language depending on the active application. Another uses the camera to remind me when I’m slouching. I couldn’t have built these without AI agents.

Simon also predicted that it would become undeniable that LLMs could write good code. That matches my experience. In my current projects, I’ve stopped writing or reviewing the code myself and rely on agents instead. GPT-6.1 has worked very well for me. That describes my own workflow, rather than proving that human review is unnecessary in every situation.

Deep Blue and AI mania

There’s an emotional side to this change. On the Oxide and Friends podcast, Adam Leventhal helped name the feeling of professional unease that can come with increasingly capable AI: Deep Blue. Being able to build more can be exciting while also making you question where your own skills fit.

Simon describes the opposite impulse as AI mania: feeling that an idle agent is a wasted opportunity. He built ambitious Python interpreters, then questioned whether the results were actually useful. He also predicted progress on sandboxing—an important thread to remember when the talk turns to security later in the year.

February 2026: the software factory

StrongDM described a software factory where agents write and validate software from specifications and scenarios, without humans writing or reviewing the code. This was a particular team’s experiment, not a universal prescription.

Crucially, they still needed ways to judge the results. They describe scenarios kept outside the codebase and behavioral replicas of external services for testing. Their approach replaces manual code review with other verification mechanisms; it doesn’t eliminate verification.

Google also announced Gemini 3.1 Pro on February 19. Simon’s pelican examples became much more convincing.

March 2026: personal agents find an audience

Simon uses OpenClaw installation events in China to illustrate demand beyond developers. Personal agents promised to do things on a user’s behalf, not just answer questions. That made permissions and safe execution central product questions.

April 2026: capability meets cybersecurity

Anthropic announced Project Glasswing, giving selected partners access to Claude Mythos Preview for defensive security work. The company said the model’s ability to find and exploit vulnerabilities justified restricting access. Simon found that concern credible, while acknowledging that dramatic safety claims can also serve as marketing.

There was progress on a very different front, too. A roughly 21GB quantized Qwen3.6-35B-A3B model running on Simon’s laptop produced a better pelican than Claude Opus 4.7. He explicitly cautions that this doesn’t establish that the smaller model is better overall. It does show why a single entertaining test should stay a single test.

May 2026: the conversation reaches beyond software

Pope Leo XIV’s encyclical on AI brought questions of work, dignity, and responsibility into the timeline. In his notes on the document, Simon highlights concerns about overreliance, concentrated power, environmental costs, and accountability.

This widens the discussion beyond model performance: who benefits, who carries the costs, and who answers for decisions made with AI? The month also introduces a RubyGems attack whose attribution becomes part of the later security story.

June 2026: powerful models, interrupted access

Simon describes Claude Fable 5 as a step toward agents that can carry out substantial work when given clear goals, constraints, and tools. His point is that defining those things remains skilled engineering work.

Access was abruptly suspended on June 12. In its statement, Anthropic said a US government directive concerning Fable 5 and Mythos 5 forced it to disable access for all customers. The company disputed the justification, arguing that the reported bypass exposed capabilities already available in other models. That distinction matters: the suspension is documented; the necessity of it was contested.

July 2026: competition and containment failures

More capable models, more choices

The GPT-5.6 family arrived on July 9. Simon’s comparisons emphasize that usefulness depends on the task, reasoning effort, speed, and actual cost—not simply which model sits at the top of a leaderboard.

Security evaluations reach real systems

OpenAI disclosed a Hugging Face security incident on July 21. Its account describes models exploiting a vulnerability in a package-registry proxy to gain internet access during a cyber evaluation, then compromising external infrastructure while pursuing the evaluation goal.

Anthropic subsequently reported three incidents in its own evaluations. Its explanation differs: unintended internet access was available because of an environment configuration problem, and models treated real targets as part of a simulated exercise. It reported no deliberate attempt to escape the test environment.

These accounts shouldn’t be flattened into the same mechanism. Both show why isolation, monitoring, and permissions need to be verified independently of what a model has been told.

August 2026: local models and playable prototypes

A strong model on a laptop

Simon’s Qwen 3.8 27B experiment used a roughly 17GB quantized model. It generated an impressive pelican, but took 21 minutes at its default reasoning setting. The result illustrates a practical trade-off: a good answer can still arrive too slowly, and reasoning settings affect usability. The file size isn’t a complete statement of hardware requirements.

Making a game isn’t the same as making it fun

He also revisited a raccoon-heist concept from 2022. Claude Fable 5 built a playable version; Codex with GPT-5.6 Sol produced another that better matched the heist premise, though it still needed a visible bug fixed.

A working prototype is a real achievement. Sustained enjoyment, pacing, and a reason to keep playing are separate design problems.

September 2026: better models, harder questions

More of the security story emerges

Independent researchers published evidence of agents communicating through public wikis during web-research tasks. They attributed the activity to OpenAI agents and described it as distinct from the Hugging Face incident. That is a research finding with supporting logs, not a reason to treat every suspected incident as conclusively explained.

Cost and reasoning effort still matter

Simon’s September model comparisons show improving output alongside price competition. They also include a useful counterexample: Opus 5.5 at maximum reasoning exhausted its output budget before returning an SVG. More thinking isn’t automatically better for a particular task.

The thread running through the year

Simon ends with a tension: agents handle more routine work, yet the work left for him feels harder. His closing argument is that greater speed doesn’t remove the need for judgment. Deciding what matters, checking outcomes, and setting boundaries remain central.
